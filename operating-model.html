Running a system 80+ teams chose

With no mandate, the system only grew if teams trusted it. So I ran it like a product with customers who could leave: clear rules for what gets built, a way for anyone to contribute, steady releases, and real numbers on who was using it.

Role
Product owner
Team
7 to 10 developers, designers, QA and a digital architect
Years
Sep 2021 to Jul 2026
Reach
80+ product teams, 13 ministries

I designed the processes on this page and ran them with the team. Developers on the team built the components and packages.

Deciding what to build

I designed a prioritization framework that scores each request on work type, reach, urgency, risk and size, with modifiers for the team asking. Scores fall into four priority bands, so partner teams could see why one request came before theirs.

I also set up four tiers of stakeholder communication: practitioners, adoption enablers, alignment partners and executives. Each tier got the updates it needed, and I reported monthly to the Deputy Minister of Technology and Innovation.

Letting teams contribute

  • Work tagged as ready for contribution in the public GitHub backlog, with a contribution guide on design.alberta.ca and support from the core team.
  • A monthly recognition for top contributors. One developer outside the team made 9 contributions.
  • A shared bug triage model, built with a partner team, that scores each bug and sets clear ownership between the system team and the product team.
  • Twice-weekly drop-in hours, a structured bug form and a support channel.

Shipping versions and moving teams forward

We shipped 68 production releases of the web component library from November 2022, about one every 20 days, alongside React, Angular and design token packages.

Before the major version update in April 2026, I ran an early adopter program with 7 teams across Justice, Child Care, Education and Housing. Their feedback reshaped the documentation and migration guidance before release. It also showed that page-by-page migration wasn't possible because of how tokens and styles were structured, so we changed the support plan before launch rather than after.

I wrote the migration strategy: three pathways for teams at different readiness levels, named accountabilities for each role, a decision flow and a tracking workbook. Launch was a four-week rollout that trained designers first, then developers.

Measuring adoption instead of guessing

Leadership set a 60% migration target with no definition behind it. I defined which projects counted, using minimum package versions and recent activity, and renegotiated what the target measured. Then we put a scanner in place across the government's GitHub organization, so adoption was reported every month instead of estimated.

What changed

  • 80+ product teamsAdopted the system across 13 ministries, without a mandate.
  • 1M+ package downloadsWeb component downloads grew every year, including the year of the major version update.
  • 24% of eligible projectsMoved to the new version within three months, against a 60% target. Tracking by code stack showed which teams needed more help.
